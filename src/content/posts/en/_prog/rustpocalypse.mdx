Everyone has heard how **blazingly fast Rust ⚡** is, how every project is being rewritten in it, and how AI tools have become a major force pushing that along.
I wrote this post to unpack why switching to another language rarely makes sense.

## Motivation and Background
I'm a developer who has worked with Java and Minecraft optimization since **2016**.
I've tackled plenty of challenges over the years, including some deeply troubled codebases.
Back then Rust wasn't mainstream yet, so we had to solve things with what we had.
Honestly, I don't like the idea of porting Minecraft to another language.
Why? 
Why hasn't Minecraft been officially rewritten in Rust yet?
Let's dig in.

## Porting to Another Language
Jokes aside, deciding to migrate a codebase to a different language is a last-resort move.
Minecraft's bottlenecks aren't about the language—they stem from architectural decisions and how carefully systems were designed.  
A good example from my own experience: simply caching hashes on [CompoundTag](https://mcsrc.dev/2/26.3/net/minecraft/nbt/CompoundTag#L410) in the VimeMC project cut a huge share of allocations and CPU load.

On top of that, the cost of migrating, learning a new ecosystem, and debugging brand-new code outweighs common sense.

## The AI Trap
AI assistants smooth over some of the friction around Rust's borrow checker and boilerplate, yet they don't erase the real cost of a rewrite.

### A Staged Argument
**Ivan** — "We decided to rewrite the project in Rust, look at these projected gains. Granted, it'll cost X."  
**Pyotr Semyonovich** — "Why did you choose a rewrite over optimizing what we have? All our engineers know Java."  
**Ivan** — "Java is slow—just look at these pause-time charts[^1]!"  
**Pyotr Semyonovich** — "I see long pauses. How does eliminating them solve our bottleneck, and why would another language be required?"  
**Sashok** — "Actually, those pauses are a deliberate trade-off to drain queues faster. We can swap to a different garbage collector that avoids pauses like that."  
**Pyotr Semyonovich** — "Settled—rewrite rejected. New task: optimize the existing application."

What happened here? 
Why did Sashok ruin the fun? 
Because that's reality. 
With so many teams rewriting projects in Rust out of passion rather than necessity, it snowballed into an industry trend.
AI makes this worse by subtly altering architecture and patterns on the fly during a rewrite while the developer stops paying attention to what's actually changing.

### Bun
Even a well-known project like **Bun** stepped on that rake[^2].
Sure, AI got remarkably close to completing the rewrite almost autonomously.
Yet there were plenty of caveats:
1. Setting up an extraordinarily complex workflow, rules, and test harnesses.
2. The effort took **11 days**, which is awfully slow relative to the resources poured in.
3. Project cost wasn't even a constraint because budgets were effectively unlimited[^3].
4. Even with all that investment, countless hard-won fixes, edge-case solutions, and features were lost.
5. The result still wasn't production-ready—it took months of additional polishing afterward.

## Wrapping Up
It becomes clear why Microsoft knows how complex their codebase is—and seeing how severe the bugs in Bedrock still are[^4], there's little room for doubt.
The issue isn't the migration target; it's how hard it is to faithfully reproduce a complex system.
Meanwhile, countless community efforts to rewrite Minecraft in Rust keep hitting the same wall and getting stuck in perpetual <Tooltip term="Work In Progress">WIP</Tooltip> purgatory.

And I haven't even touched on losing an entire ecosystem of mods and plugins just because someone picked a different language.  
Sure, everyone could migrate too—but that's a whole different story.
