## Introduction to Difficulty
I used to think the problem of choosing difficulty levels in games was solved long ago—play however you want! 
Not quite: online games enforce a fixed difficulty, and not nearly all single-player games offer a choice either. 
Some even mock you for picking an easier mode.

**This raises a few interesting questions:**
1. Who does the game exist for?
2. What does the game exist for?
3. Should I keep playing?

> [!NOTE]
> Difficulty is a concept that exists in every genre, whether it's an online shooter, a visual novel, or an <Tooltip term="Role-Playing Game">RPG</Tooltip>.

## A Concrete Example
Recently I was playing *Umineko When They Cry*[^1], and you'd think: **what kind of difficulty could there possibly be in a visual novel?**

I thought the same thing—yet I got so absorbed in the story that I started burning out and falling prey to the author's manipulation and abuse[^2].
Of course, I tried solving the mystery fairly, but halfway through <Tooltip term="Alliance of the Golden Witch">Episode 4</Tooltip> I realized that if I didn't find out the answer, I wouldn't finish the game at all.

**Yes, I cheated!** Still, on the other side of the scale were my well-being and keeping a positive relationship with the game.
My answers to those interesting questions:
1. **Who does the game exist for?** 
— For me.
2. **What does the game exist for?** 
— So the author can speak their mind, and I can enjoy it.
3. **Should I keep playing?** 
— Definitely, on my own terms.

In the end, I progressed much further and genuinely enjoyed watching the plot unfold, already knowing what was going on underneath and how the author veiled their ideas. 
It doesn't even compare to the misery before uncovering the facts.

## Cheating
Just like nature itself, games tend to balance themselves automatically, regardless of the developers. 
In nature you get predators; in games you get cheaters.

I don't think you can call predators villains—otherwise herbivores would strip nature bare.
I feel the same way about cheaters. Cheaters appear either from a lack of engagement with the loop or from overly punishing gameplay difficulty.

Naturally, game companies start fighting cheaters. 
And as human experience shows, that only degrades the habitat[^3].
Dedicated players who actually care about the game—and I see cheaters as some of the most invested players—want to stick around, yet disagree with specific design choices; it's a kind of quiet (or sometimes loud[^4]) protest.

So yes, the "cheater problem" isn't really a problem—it's the logical endpoint of players optimizing away friction.

### What should competitive games do?
I don't think they need to overreact to it. 
Of course, they have to protect infrastructure so malicious actors can't crash servers or cause chaos.

Improving gameplay conditions and building proper matchmaking that naturally pairs cheaters with cheaters by effective skill will do the rest.
A cheater won't have any incentive to play at the skill bracket a cheat grants when the game already feels fair at their level. 
There will always be exceptions, of course, and exceptions are what drive change.

### What about single-player games?
Under this header I'll include not just single-player titles, but also class selection, abilities, and similar systems present in online games too.

Here's the blunt truth: classes, abilities, and playstyles **SHOULD NOT** dictate how hard it is to beat a game unless it was explicitly designed around that (meaning the player knows upfront).

Here is my gameplay loop using <Tooltip term="The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim">Skyrim</Tooltip> as an example:

1. **Class choice** — I want to play as a warrior and charge straight into combat.
2. **Friction** — The game artificially stretches things out with tedious grinding.
3. **Autonomy** — Developer console / cheats as a tool to trim the chore and return to the fun.

I hate sitting around regretting my build choice later, because my goal is to relax in a game—not agonize over "how will I even play this a couple of hours from now?"

## Takeaways
In my personal experience, a game should adapt to the player, not the other way around.
When a game refuses to do that, it pushes me to edit config files, dig through forums, and break immersion.

If I accidentally make the game too easy—well, my mistake, it happens. 
Even so, this mindset saved more than one playthrough for me: **OMORI, Atomic Heart, The Witcher III: Wild Hunt**.
And yes, nearly every game gets this treatment—not because I'm a **casual**, but rather because I'm a **cozy gamer**[^5].

That's also why I avoid competitive games: cheating there is far too costly (both morally and practically), and the payoff is questionable.
It's also where my respect comes from for people who "revive" a game for themselves by finishing the balancing work developers left behind.
It's worth noting that for the exact same reason I skipped **Elden Ring**—its whole point is the difficulty, which I would have bypassed anyway—and I have zero regrets about choosing not to play a game meant to hurt.

Sure, this article drifted into talking about cheats, since that happens to be my favorite way to bring fun back into a game.

> [!NOTE]
> Feel free to replace every occurrence of "cheats" with "modifications" to soften the emotional baggage.

I believe a game should be enjoyable; once it stops being fun, it turns into mechanical busywork for no clear reason.
For me, that means steady pacing without sudden difficulty spikes, no forced constraints when choices exist, and rich storytelling. 
For others, it may be something else. 
What matters is that the game fulfills its purpose.
